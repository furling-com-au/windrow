The peninsula's one independent grain gateway is shut inland and up for sale

Until 2019 an Eyre Peninsula grower had nowhere to deliver bulk grain outside one company's network. Then T-Ports opened Lucky Bay. Six years on, its two inland sites are closed for the season, its owner is running a sale process, and the regulator's finding that the incumbent "owns the vast majority of storage" upcountry has not been retested since.

Tier 1 · regulator's finding
Viterra has a dominant position upcountry on the Eyre Peninsula, where it owns the vast majority of storage. ACCC, final determinations on Viterra's Bulk Wheat Code exemption applications, 2021

That was written when the peninsula had three independent receival points. It has one now.

The count behind that sentence is a floor, not a census. On the operator's own weekly harvest reports for 2025/26 we can evidence at least 22 Bunge receival points operating on the Eyre Peninsula, and one that is not Bunge's — T-Ports' Lucky Bay. We have not found another. We are not in a position to say there is no other: nothing in this work systematically audits non-Bunge receival capacity, and at least three sites the operator has itself named in earlier seasons are missing from our list.

That last figure is the one the peninsula's growers would notice first, and it runs the other way. Before 2019 there was no independent option at all — not a diminished one, none. T-Ports' arrival is the only time competition has increased here, and it is still present.

How far is the alternative

Tier 2 · geometry Measuring drive time from each production cell to the nearest independent receival point — road distances and site locations only, no simulation and no fitted parameter — about two-thirds of Eyre Peninsula grain production sits more than 90 minutes from a non-Bunge delivery point, against a median 16.5-minute run to the nearest Bunge site and a 99th percentile of 58.3 minutes.

This is a distance to one port. Because Lucky Bay is now the only independent point, the figure is a pure function of how far each grower is from it. It would read exactly the same if Bunge operated three sites on the peninsula or two hundred, and it rose when the entrant closed sites, not because of anything the incumbent did. It should not be read as a measure of Bunge's reach.

No threshold was designated in advance. Two earlier justifications for singling out 90 minutes were withdrawn on 31 August 2026 when neither survived checking, so the whole column is printed instead. The finding holds at every row: even at the most generous threshold tested, two hours' drive, 39.5% of production has no non-Bunge option.

Two sensitivities, both reproducible and neither cancelling: 66.8% at the published coordinates; 61.5–67.3% across the ±5 km tolerance on Lucky Bay's town-level coordinate; 54.5–77.2% if the assumed road speeds are 15% out in either direction. A non-uniform speed error is not bounded by that band. In one sentence: about two-thirds on our published road speeds, and between roughly a half and three-quarters across the sensitivities we tested.

The 2025/26 closures are published: T-Ports' own harvest page carries "Site closed for 25/26 season" under both the Lock and the Kimba headings, with Lucky Bay and Wallaroo carrying no such notice. T-Ports closed its own sites — this was the independent operator's decision, not something done to it — and the notice is season-specific. Nothing here establishes that either bunker has left the market permanently.

The 2023/24 cells are marked unverified deliberately. T-Ports publishes no season-by-season intake, and the only per-season operating evidence this work holds is Viterra and Bunge's weekly harvest reports, which never name a competitor's sites. We know the bunkers existed with published capacities; we cannot show from any published source that they took grain that season.

Independent capacity on the peninsula peaked in 2023/24. Any comparison drawn against that year is a comparison against the maximum, not against a long-run norm.

The 2019 rationalisation, and what it was not

In June 2019 Viterra announced the closure of 17 up-country sites statewide, six of them on the Eyre Peninsula: Minnipa, Kyancutta, Cungena, Waddikee, Kielpa and Wharminda. Two of the six — Minnipa and Kyancutta — had operated the previous season. The other four had not. Bunge acquired Viterra in July 2025 and had no part in these decisions.

Viterra's published explanation, from operations manager Michael Hill:

Since 2010, the average truck size has increased by 30 per cent and those bigger trucks are delivering to our bigger sites. Stock Journal, Alisha Fogden, 6 June 2019

The company also cited changing delivery patterns and quality and food-safety requirements, and said the closed sites represented less than 2% of total receivals on a five-year average.

Grain trains ceased running on the peninsula in May 2019, the month before. No published source connects the two, and this analysis cannot separate them either: both events are Eyre Peninsula-only and fall in the same season, so they are perfectly collinear in the data. Nor is there a visible effect to attribute — the peninsula's share of network receivals shows no step across the 2019 boundary, averaging 37.3% before and 38.4% after.

One correction to the closure record, found in the operator's own later reports: Cungena took grain again in November 2022, three seasons after being listed as closed permanently.

Why it matters this year

  • 27 Apr 2021

    The ACCC exempts Viterra's two Port Adelaide terminals from Parts 3–6 of the Bulk Wheat Code and refuses exemption for Wallaroo and Port Giles.

  • 20 Jul 2021

    It refuses Port Lincoln and Thevenard, which remain subject to the full Code. On Port Lincoln the ACCC found Lucky Bay imposes "a material level of competitive constraint… in certain regions of its catchment area", while "the constraint imposed in other regions is likely limited, in particular most of the Lower Eyre Peninsula". It also noted that competition in SA had increased in recent seasons, and that distance "is only one factor".

  • 10 Oct – 17 Dec 2024

    The ACCC reviews Viterra's proposed purchase of five Grainflow sites and a mobile ship loader, and discontinues on 17 December after Viterra advised on 13 December that it would not proceed. No findings were announced. None of the five sites is on the Eyre Peninsula; peninsula parties engaged because of the acquirer's statewide position. T-Ports opposed. Grain Producers SA surveyed growers and put their concerns to the ACCC without taking a position.

  • 2 Jul 2025

    Bunge completes its merger with Viterra. The ACCC assessed and did not oppose the global transaction in December 2023. On 21 August 2025, Viterra Operations Pty Ltd is renamed Bunge Operations Pty Ltd.

  • Jul – Dec 2025

    T-Ports goes to market through Nash Advisory. Media reports put the value at about $250m, unconfirmed by the vendor or its adviser; a separate report cites offers of more than $200m. From December it is offered as a package with Australian Grain Export and was, as at 3 December 2025, unsold, with the adviser reporting advanced discussions with a couple of unnamed parties.

  • Sep – Nov 2024

    The Wheat Port Code's automatic sunset is deferred 24 months, to 1 October 2026, so the Code is in force today. The ACCC had submitted that it "should be allowed to sunset". The department's review found it no longer fit for purpose and recommended an industry-managed code developed by Grain Trade Australia; consultation opened 7 November 2024.

  • Jul – Aug 2026

    Bunge puts six surplus SA sites to public tender through Elders, including Murdinga on the Eyre Peninsula — a "past site" with 23,400 t of storage that has not taken grain in any of the last ten seasons. Binding offers closed 21 August 2026; the outcome is unpublished.

The question a regulator would have to answer, if the peninsula's one independent gateway changed hands, is narrower than any of this: for how much of the region's grain does a genuine alternative exist, and at what distance. That question is easier to answer than it was in 2021, because there is now only one alternative to measure.

What this does not claim

  • This counts delivery points, not buyers. It measures physical receival points and who operates them. Growers also sell to traders, mills, feedlots and container packers through warehoused and delivered-site contracts at Bunge sites, hold grain on farm, and sell into channels no receival point in this count represents. Handling concentration is not buyer concentration. not measured
  • No absence claims. "We have found one" is not "there is one". No systematic audit of non-Bunge receival capacity was performed, and the geometry is clipped to an Eyre Peninsula study area that excludes options beyond it — including T-Ports' own Wallaroo terminal — by construction rather than by assessment. An unlisted non-Bunge site would lower the 66.8% figure. not measured
  • No motive. Nothing here establishes why any site closed, and no inference of strategy should be drawn from the sequence of events. Where a company has published its own explanation, that explanation is quoted above.
  • No queue or turnaround times. A simulation of the peninsula's grain logistics exists behind this work. Its operational outputs failed validation and are not used for any claim on this page. excluded
  • No price effect. No claim that concentration has moved farm-gate prices. Testing that needs a cash-bid history that is not published and that we have only begun to collect. excluded
  • An incomplete roster. Tooligie, Cowell and Mangalo are named receiving grain in the operator's own reports and are absent from our site list; Murdinga is absent as a closed site; Cungena's closure record is contradicted; and Kapinnie sits inside the 22 on an off-season segregations read rather than on 2025/26 receival evidence. The counts are floors.

Method and sources

Every claim is one of two kinds, marked throughout:

  • Tier 1 — published fact: regulatory determinations, company announcements, the operator's own weekly harvest reports, and the trade record. No modelling.
  • Tier 2 — geometry over road distances and site locations. No calibrated or fitted parameter.

A third tier — simulated queues, truck movements and site stocks — was tested against two real network changes and failed validation: the peninsula's published receivals are reported as a single regional total, so no observed geography exists at a fine enough grain to check a redistribution against. Nothing from that tier appears here. The test and its failure are documented rather than omitted.

Reproducibility. The 66.8% level comes from pipeline/r8_choice_geometry.py; the 61.5–67.3% coordinate envelope comes from pipeline/r8a_coord_sensitivity.py. These are different scripts and the distinction matters: r8's own coordinate sweep holds the 2025/26 independent set fixed and therefore cannot test the coordinate at all. Inputs are data/processed/sites.geojson, data/processed/roads.graph.json, app/public/data/parcels.json and the PIRSA district production behind the tonne weights.

Principal sources. ACCC Bulk Wheat Code determinations (2021) and public informal merger reviews register; Viterra and Bunge weekly harvest reports, ten seasons; T-Ports harvest and site pages, archived 31 August 2026; Stock Journal and Grain Central reporting, cited by author and date above; Bunge Global SEC filings; Flinders Port Holdings monthly statistics; PIRSA district production; OpenStreetMap road network.
